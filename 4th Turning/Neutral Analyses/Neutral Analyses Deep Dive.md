# 4th Turning: A Balanced Assessment of the Strauss-Howe Theory

*Rewritten from the evidence up in Step 13 (2026-10-17). The previous version, which was mostly meta-commentary about neutrality, is preserved at `archive/step13_originals/4th Turning/Neutral Analyses/Neutral Analyses Deep Dive.md.orig`.*

## Executive Summary

This report weighs what the Strauss-Howe generational theory gets right against where it fails, using the theory's own chronology, independent reviews of generational research, and the data tested in the other rewritten 4th Turning reports. The theory is useful as a narrative lens. It correctly points to a few real things: large cohorts can shape eras (the post-war baby boom), and American history has periodic constitutional or existential crises. As a predictive model it is weak. The saecula in the authors' own chronology run from 71 to 110 years (mean 91.8, standard deviation 16.7). Crisis turnings run from 5 to 29 years. One cycle is admitted to have no Hero generation, and the current Crisis has been dated and redated after events. Independent assessments of generational categories, from the National Academies of Sciences (2020) and Pew Research Center (2023), find that most apparent generational differences dissolve once age and period effects are separated. The fair conclusion is that the theory is a source of hypotheses, not evidence, and the Convergence model should use it that way.

## Question and scope

**Question.** On a balanced reading of the evidence, is the Strauss-Howe theory a descriptive framework that fits history, a predictive model that can be tested, or neither?

**Scope.** The theory as set out in *Generations* (1991) and *The Fourth Turning* (1997), with the dates Neil Howe currently uses. Evidence: the internal consistency of the theory's chronology; independent reviews of generational research; and the birth and trust data analysed in `Demographic Trends/Demographic Trends Deep Dive.md` and `Public Perception/Public Perception Deep Dive.md`. Not covered: the theory's use in marketing and politics, except where it bears on reception.

## Methodology

1. Take the saeculum and turning dates from the theory's published chronology and compute how regular they are. A cycle with a stable period should show low variation.
2. Review assessments of generational research by bodies with no stake in the theory.
3. Summarise the tests run in the other 4th Turning rewrites.
4. Separate the claims into three groups: supported, not supported, and not testable as stated.

## Empirical Evidence

### How regular is the cycle in the theory's own dates?

| Saeculum (Strauss-Howe dates) | Length (years) | Crisis turning | Crisis length (years) |
| :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| Reformation (1487-1594) | 107 | 1569-1594 | 25 |
| New World (1594-1704) | 110 | 1675-1704 | 29 |
| Revolutionary (1704-1794) | 90 | 1773-1794 | 21 |
| Civil War (1794-1865) | 71 | 1860-1865 | 5 |
| Great Power (1865-1946) | 81 | 1929-1946 | 17 |
| Millennial (1946-2033?) | 87 if it ends in 2033 | 2008-2033? | 25 if it ends in 2033 |

For the five completed saecula, the mean length is 91.8 years with a standard deviation of 16.7 years (range 71-110). Crisis turnings average 19.4 years with a standard deviation of 9.2 years (range 5-29). Across the 23 dated turnings in the chronology, lengths run from 5 to 30 years. A theory that says each turning lasts "about 20 years" and each saeculum "about 80-90 years" has to absorb a 39-year spread in saeculum length and a sixfold spread in crisis length. The Civil War cycle is a known anomaly. As summarised in the Wikipedia account of the theory, the authors explain it by saying the war came "about ten years too early", with the result that "there is no hero generation in this Cycle". An explanation added after the fact for a cycle that does not fit is a warning sign for testability.

### What independent reviews of generational research find

- **National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2020).** The committee, convened at the request of the U.S. Army Research Institute, concluded that categorising workers by generation "is not supported by research". It found that differences are "more likely to reflect differences in their ages, career stages, job experiences, and general changes in society", and that "individuals from the same generation are just as likely to be different from one another as they are from individuals born in other generations."
- **Pew Research Center (2023).** After a year-long review, Pew said it would only do generational analysis where long-run data allow comparisons at the same life stage, would control for period effects and demographic change, and would often not use the standard labels. Its worked examples found that some apparent generation effects (in moving rates, for instance) were explained by other factors.

These reviews concern generational *differences* in attitudes and behaviour, not Strauss and Howe's cycle as such. But the cycle depends on generations having distinct, lasting characters (the four archetypes). If generational differences are mostly age and period effects, the archetype mechanism loses its footing.

### What the other rewritten reports found

- **Births** (Demographic Trends): the baby boom is a real cohort with sharp edges (births up 19.3% in 1946), but no other generational boundary shows in the birth record, and fertility does not track the turnings.
- **Trust** (Public Perception): trust peaked in 1964 and collapsed in the Awakening, as the theory expects. But the expected rebuilding in the claimed Crisis since 2008 has not appeared. Trust in government was 17% in September 2025.

### Sorting the claims

| Claim | Assessment |
| :--- | :--- |
| Large birth cohorts shape the eras they live through | Supported for the baby boom; generic to demography, not specific to the theory |
| American history has recurring national crises (1770s-90s, 1860s, 1930s-40s) | Widely accepted; which events count and when they start or end is the authors' choice |
| Turnings last about 20 years and saecula about 80-90 | Not supported by the theory's own dates (5-30 and 71-110 years) |
| Each generation has a lasting archetype set by when it came of age | Not supported by the NASEM and Pew reviews of generational differences |
| A Crisis began around 2008 and will resolve by the early 2030s | Not testable until after the fact; the start and end dates have been adjusted as events unfolded |

## Competing views and counterarguments

- **The case for the theory.** Supporters say it is a "pattern recognition" framework, not physics, and should be judged on whether it helps people anticipate the mood of an era. Some reviewers praised its ambition and reach. On that standard, supporters point to the 1997 book's expectation of a national crisis in the following decades as a hit. Critics reply that the forecast was broad enough to fit many outcomes.
- **The main criticisms.** Historians and social scientists call it deterministic and unfalsifiable. Michael Lind, reviewing *The Fourth Turning* in *The New York Times Book Review* (1997), wrote that cyclical theories "tend to end up in the Sargasso Sea of pseudoscience" and that the book "is no exception". Peter Turchin, whose own work models secular cycles mathematically, has said it is closer to prophecy than science because it forces the historical record to fit. David Greenberg (Rutgers) called it a "crackpot" theory in *Politico* (2017).
- **A middle position.** Cyclical models with explicit mechanisms and data, such as Turchin's structural-demographic theory, make claims that can be checked against wages, elite numbers and state finances. Strauss-Howe can be read as a qualitative cousin of such models. It is worth keeping as a source of hypotheses, provided each one is then tested on its own data.

## Limitations

- The regularity test uses dates chosen by the theory's authors. Other historians would draw different boundaries, which would change the numbers but would not create a regular cycle.
- The chronology comes from a Wikipedia table that follows Strauss and Howe's books. It was checked for internal consistency, not against the original pages.
- The NASEM and Pew reviews concern generational differences in general, mainly in the workforce and survey attitudes. They do not test the turnings directly.
- "Balanced" does not mean splitting the difference. Where the evidence points one way, this report says so.

## Conclusion

Read neutrally, the Strauss-Howe theory is a stimulating narrative of American history with a weak empirical core. Its regularity claims fail on its own dates. Its archetype mechanism conflicts with the best current reviews of generational research. Its forecast of the current Crisis can be adjusted to fit almost any outcome. The Convergence model should not treat a "Fourth Turning" as an input with explanatory weight. It can use the theory to suggest indicators, such as cross-party institutional trust or the timing of constitutional settlements, and then rely on those indicators' own data.
