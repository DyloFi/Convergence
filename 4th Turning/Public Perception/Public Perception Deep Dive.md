# 4th Turning: What Seven Decades of Trust Polling Show

*Rewritten from the evidence up in Step 13 (2026-10-17). The previous version, which generalised about polling without citing survey series, is preserved at `archive/step13_originals/4th Turning/Public Perception/Public Perception Deep Dive.md.orig`.*

## Executive Summary

The Strauss-Howe model predicts that public trust in institutions peaks during a High, is attacked during an Awakening, erodes during an Unraveling, and is rebuilt as a Crisis comes to a head. The longest consistent US series, the question "How much of the time do you trust the government in Washington to do what is right?" (asked since 1958 by the National Election Studies and later Pew), fits the first half of this pattern and not the second. Trust peaked at 77% in 1964, at the end of the High, then collapsed during the Awakening to roughly a quarter by 1980. In the Unraveling (1984-2008) it recovered several times, reaching 60% in October 2001. Pew reports it has not been higher than 30% since 2007 (on its smoothed trend; one October 2008 NES reading was 31%), and in September 2025 it was 17%, one of the lowest readings on record. Gallup's average confidence across 14 institutions shows the same low: 26% in 2023, 28% in 2024, against a range of 31-43% before 2022. If 2008 began a Crisis, the trust data so far show the trough the model expects for an Unraveling, not the rebuilding it expects for a Crisis. The polls also show a strong partisan pattern: since the 1970s, supporters of the party holding the White House trust the government more. That pattern points to period and political effects rather than a generational rhythm.

## Question and scope

**Question.** Do long-running US surveys of trust in government and confidence in institutions follow the High → Awakening → Unraveling → Crisis sequence the Strauss-Howe model predicts, and when did the current decline begin?

**Scope.** United States, 1958-2025. Two series: (1) trust in the federal government ("just about always" or "most of the time"), compiled by Pew from NES, Gallup, ABC/Washington Post, CBS/New York Times, CNN and its own surveys; (2) Gallup's average confidence in 14 institutions tracked since 1993. Not covered: trust in other people (social trust), trust in other countries, or attitudes within generations.

## Methodology

Each reading is placed in the turning it falls in, using Strauss and Howe's dates: High 1946-1964, Awakening 1964-1984, Unraveling 1984-2008, Crisis from 2008. For each turning I note the level, the direction of change and the turning points. No smoothing is applied beyond Pew's own three-survey moving average, which is reported only where stated. The question is whether the direction of change matches the model, not whether a statistical cycle can be fitted. About 67 years of data cannot test an 80-year cycle formally.

## Empirical Evidence

### Trust in the federal government, by turning

| Turning | Readings (individual polls) | Direction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| High (to 1964) | 73% (1958), 77% (1964) | High and stable; the series peaks in 1964 |
| Awakening (1964-1984) | 65% (1966), 62% (1968), 54% (1970), 53% (1972), 36% (1974), 25% (NES, 1980) | Steep fall, through Vietnam, Watergate and stagflation |
| Unraveling (1984-2008) | 44% (NES, 1984), 47% (CBS/NYT, March 1991), 17% (Gallup, June 1994), 60% (Gallup, October 2001), 31% (NES, 2008) | Low baseline with several sharp recoveries (mid-1980s, Gulf War, late 1990s, after 9/11) |
| Crisis (2008-) | 31% (NES, October 2008), then no reading above 29%; 24% (2021), 16% (2023), 22% (2024), 17% (February and September 2025) | Flat at historic lows |

Source: Pew Research Center, *Public Trust in Government: 1958-2025* (fact sheet updated December 4, 2025).

### Confidence across institutions

Gallup has tracked 14 institutions every year since 1993. Their average confidence ranged from 43% in the early 2000s to 26% in 2023. The 2024 reading of 28% was the third year in a row below 30%. Before 2022 the average had always been between 31% and 43%. Gallup also reports that Democrats' average confidence fell to a new low in 2025 in a trend going back to 1979, while Republicans' rose.

### The partisan pattern

Pew finds that since the 1970s, trust has been consistently higher among supporters of the party that holds the presidency. The swing after the 2024 election shows how large this is. Democrats' trust went from 35% (May 2024) to 9% (September 2025). Republicans' went from 11% to 26%. A generational or saecular mood would affect everyone. A swing that reverses with control of the White House is a political period effect.

### Reading the evidence against the claim

- **Consistent with the model:** the 1964 peak at the end of the High and the steep fall during the Awakening.
- **Partly consistent:** the low baseline of the Unraveling. But the recoveries in 1984, 1991 and 2001-2002 show the decline was not a steady unravelling.
- **Not yet consistent:** the model's Crisis rebuilds trust in collective institutions (the reference case is the 1940s). Seventeen years into the claimed Crisis, trust in the federal government is at or near its lowest. Either the Crisis has not reached the turning point where trust rebuilds, or the period since 2008 is not a Crisis in the model's sense. The data cannot yet tell these apart. Defenders can always move the climax later, which is a reason to be cautious about the theory's testability (see the Neutral Analyses report).

## Competing views and counterarguments

- **Events and performance.** Political scientists generally explain trust by government performance and salient events: war, scandal, economic conditions and rally effects after attacks. That account explains the 1974 Watergate drop, the 1991 and 2001 rallies and the 2008 recession low without needing a cycle.
- **Polarisation.** The size of the partisan swing suggests that negative partisanship, not generational mood, now drives much of the variation.
- **The case for the model.** One fair reading is that the 1964 peak and the long decline afterwards fit Strauss and Howe's account of an Awakening followed by an Unraveling better than they fit pure event-driven noise. That broad shape is real. What the data do not show is the specific Crisis-era recovery the model predicts.
- **International evidence** (not analysed here) would help. If trust fell at the same time in countries with different "saecular" histories, a US-specific generational cycle becomes a weaker explanation.

## Limitations

- **Mode change.** Pew's data from 2020 on come from its online American Trends Panel. Earlier data come from telephone surveys run by several organisations. Pew notes that respondents can answer differently depending on how they are interviewed, so levels before and after 2020 are not perfectly comparable.
- **Mixed sources.** Before 1976 the series is sparse NES readings. Afterwards it combines several pollsters, with different samples and timing.
- **One question.** "Trust the government in Washington" is a narrow measure. Gallup's institutional average helps but starts in 1993 for a consistent set of 14 institutions.
- **No pre-1958 data,** so the previous Crisis (1929-1946), the model's main reference case, cannot be measured on the same scale.
- **No cohort breakdown here.** This report does not separate age, period and cohort effects (see `4th Turning/Research Notes.md`).

## Conclusion

Trust in the US federal government has been low since the mid-1970s and at historic lows since 2007, with a sharp partisan swing after each change of administration. The 1964 peak and the fall during the Awakening fit the Strauss-Howe sequence. The absence of any Crisis-era recovery does not. For the Convergence model, the useful finding is the measured level and trend (low, partisan and volatile), not confirmation of a turning. A sustained rise in cross-party trust would be the first observable sign of the "rebuilding" the model predicts, and is worth tracking as an indicator.
