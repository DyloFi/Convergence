# Changing World Orders Overview

## Executive Summary
This master overview provides an exhaustive synthesis of the *Changing World Orders* research library within the Convergence initiative. Grounded in Ray Dalio's *Big Cycle* framework (Dalio 2021), Robert Gilpin's Hegemonic Stability Theory (Gilpin 1981), Immanuel Wallerstein's World-Systems Analysis (Wallerstein 1974, 2004), Graham Allison's Thucydides Trap framework (Allison 2017), and A.F.K. Organski's Power Transition Theory (Organski & Kugler 1980), this directory systematically investigates how global superpowers rise, achieve structural peak dominance, experience internal institutional and financial decay, and are ultimately displaced during macro-hegemonic transitions.

Macro-geopolitical shifts do not occur randomly; they follow recurring, structural patterns driven by interconnected economic, monetary, military, and institutional lifespans averaging roughly 250 years (Dalio 2021). The central thesis of this research corpus is that hegemon decline is primarily an internal phenomenon—triggered by unsustainable debt accumulation, money monetization, wealth polarization, and loss of productive competitiveness—which subsequently creates systemic vulnerabilities exploited by rising challenger powers.

## 1. Theoretical Foundations & Macro Framework Synthesis

The changing world order is governed by seven dominant International Relations (IR) and International Political Economy (IPE) paradigms:

1. **Ray Dalio's Big Cycle Model**: Evaluates empire strength via 18 empirical metrics across 6 distinct stages of internal order/disorder and external alignment (Dalio 2021).
2. **Graham Allison's Thucydides Trap**: Analyzes structural conflict risks when a rising power threatens to displace a ruling power, identifying 16 historical cases over 500 years (12 resulting in kinetic war) (Allison 2017).
3. **Robert Gilpin & Charles Kindleberger's Hegemonic Stability Theory (HST)**: Posits that global economic stability requires a single dominant hegemon to provide international public goods (open markets, stable reserve currency, security of global commons) (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981).
4. **Immanuel Wallerstein's World-Systems Theory**: Maps hegemonic cycles through a three-phase sequence of dominance: Agricultural/Industrial Production $\rightarrow$ Commercial Trade $\rightarrow$ Financial Leadership (Wallerstein 1974; Braudel 1984).
5. **A.F.K. Organski's Power Transition Theory**: Demonstrates that global conflict risk spikes when a dissatisfied challenger reaches power parity (80%–120% of hegemon capacity) (Organski & Kugler 1980).
6. **Halford Mackinder's Heartland Theory**: Explains Eurasian landpower dynamics and the strategic imperative of controlling the Eurasian "pivot area" against maritime containment (Mackinder 1904).
7. **Alfred Thayer Mahan's Sea Power Theory**: Establishes that hegemonic dominance depends on blue-water naval power and control of global maritime chokepoints (Mahan 1890).

## 5. Key Empirical Research Findings

1. **Financialization Autumn**: As productive competitiveness wanes due to wage cost expansion and capital flight, hegemonic powers shift from manufacturing leadership to financial services and credit creation (Wallerstein 1974; Braudel 1984).
2. **Sovereign Debt Monetization Thresholds**: When sovereign debt exceeds 100% of GDP and debt service costs surpass defense spending, central bank monetization accelerates structural debasement (Dalio 2021).
3. **Multipolar Fragmentation**: Transition periods trigger non-linear fragmentation of trade settlement mechanisms (CIPS, mBridge), reducing dependence on single-clearing architectures like SWIFT (Allison 2017).
4. **Asymmetric Escalation Dynamics**: Competitions advance across 5 synchronized warfronts: (1) Trade, (2) Technology, (3) Geopolitical/Proxy, (4) Capital/Financial, and (5) Kinetic Military (Allison 2017; Mearsheimer 2001).
