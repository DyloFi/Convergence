# International Relations Theoretical Frameworks for Hegemonic Transitions

## Executive Summary — Theoreticals
This research monograph provides an exhaustive comparative breakdown of the primary theoretical frameworks in International Relations (IR) and International Political Economy (IPE) governing world orders and hegemonic shifts. It systematically analyzes seven core academic paradigms: Realism & Offensive Realism (Mearsheimer 2001; Waltz 1979), Liberal Institutionalism (Keohane 1984; Ikenberry 2001), World-Systems Theory (Wallerstein 1974; Braudel 1984), Hegemonic Stability Theory & the Kindleberger Trap (Kindleberger 1973; Gilpin 1981), Power Transition Theory (Organski & Kugler 1980), Mackinder's Heartland Theory (Mackinder 1904), and Mahan's Sea Power Theory (Mahan 1890). Furthermore, it integrates Graham Allison's Thucydides Trap empirical framework (Allison 2017) to evaluate transition risks, sovereign debt monetization, alliance dynamics, and structural conflict probabilities.

## 2. Exhaustive Analysis of the Seven Theoretical Paradigms (Theoretical Frameworks)

### 2.1 Realism and Offensive Realism (Mearsheimer, Waltz)
- **Core Mechanics**: Structural Realism asserts that the international system is defined by anarchy—the absence of a centralized global sovereign. States are rational actors whose primary imperative is survival. Offensive Realism (Mearsheimer 2001) posits that states cannot be certain of other states' intentions, compelling them to maximize relative power and seek regional hegemony as the ultimate security guarantee.
- **Application to Changing Orders**: A rising challenger power (such as China) seeks to establish regional dominance in East Asia and expel the incumbent hegemon (the United States) from its immediate security sphere. In response, the incumbent forms containment coalitions (AUKUS, Quad), creating an acute security dilemma where defensive military preparations are perceived as offensive threats (Mearsheimer 2001; Allison 2017).

### 2.2 Liberal Institutionalism & Constitutional Order Building (Keohane, Ikenberry)
- **Core Mechanics**: Rejects the strict realist assumption that anarchy precludes durable cooperation. Keohane (1984) demonstrates that international regimes and institutions lower transaction costs, reduce information asymmetry, and facilitate focal points for coordination. Ikenberry (2001, 2011) expands this into "Constitutional Order Building," arguing that post-1945 US hegemony succeeded because it bound its own power within multilateral institutions (UN, IMF, World Bank, NATO, GATT/WTO), making Western order "easy to join and hard to overturn."
- **Application to Changing Orders**: Hegemonic transition does not necessarily require total systemic destruction if institutional rules can adapt to absorb rising powers. However, when the incumbent weaponizes multilateral institutions (e.g., SWIFT disconnections, secondary sanctions), rising powers build parallel institutional architectures (BRICS Bank, NDB, SCO, CIPS) (Ikenberry 2011).

### 2.3 World-Systems Analysis & Financialization Autumn (Wallerstein, Braudel)
- **Core Mechanics**: Formulated by Immanuel Wallerstein (1974, 2004) and Fernand Braudel (1984), World-Systems Analysis divides the capitalist global economy into a structural hierarchy of Core, Semi-Periphery, and Periphery regions. Hegemonic cycles follow a rigid three-phase sequence of dominance:

- **The Financialization Autumn**: As productive competitiveness declines due to high domestic wages and capital accumulation, the hegemon shifts capital into financial speculation, credit creation, and liquid paper debt. Braudel termed this financial expansion the "autumn of hegemony"—a signal that real capital is migrating to emerging semi-peripheral production centers (Braudel 1984; Wallerstein 1974).

### 2.4 Hegemonic Stability Theory & The Kindleberger Trap (Kindleberger, Gilpin)
- **Core Mechanics**: Pioneered by Charles Kindleberger (1973) and formalized by Robert Gilpin (1981), Hegemonic Stability Theory (HST) maintains that an open, stable global economy requires a single dominant superpower to supply international public goods:
  1. Open import markets for distress goods.
  2. Stable, liquid international reserve currency.
  3. Counter-cyclical long-term lending.
  4. Security of global commercial commons and maritime trade routes.
- **The Kindleberger Trap**: Occurs when the declining hegemon is no longer capable of supplying global public goods, and the rising challenger is unwilling or unable to assume the burden—resulting in an institutional leadership vacuum, competitive devaluations, protectionism, and global depression (as observed in 1929–1939) (Kindleberger 1973).

### 2.5 Power Transition Theory & Dissatisfaction Metrics (Organski, Kugler)
- **Core Mechanics**: Developed by A.F.K. Organski (1958) and Jacek Kugler (1980), Power Transition Theory challenges balance-of-power assumptions by arguing that international peace is highest when the dominant state holds overwhelming power superiority.
- **Parity Inflection & Dissatisfaction**: Conflict risk spikes exponentially when a rapidly growing challenger reaches power parity—defined as achieving 80% to 120% of the hegemon's composite capability—and is *dissatisfied* with the existing rules, territorial allocation, and institutional status quo (Organski & Kugler 1980).

### 2.6 Halford Mackinder's Heartland Theory & Landpower Logistics
- **Core Mechanics**: Set forth in *The Geographical Pivot of History* (Mackinder 1904, 1919). Mackinder divided the globe into the "World-Island" (Afro-Eurasia), the "Heartland" (pivot area from the Volga to the Yangtze), and the "Inner/Outer Crescents."
- **Mackinder's Dictum**: "Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland commands the World-Island; who rules the World-Island commands the world."
- **Modern Geopolitical Relevance**: Explains the strategic imperative behind Eurasian land integration (China's Belt and Road Initiative, Russia-China energy pipelines, SCO expansion) as a land-based counter to Western maritime encirclement (Mackinder 1904).

### 2.7 Alfred Thayer Mahan's Sea Power Theory & Maritime Chokepoints
- **Core Mechanics**: Detailed in *The Influence of Sea Power Upon History* (Mahan 1890). Mahan argued that global hegemony is inextricably linked to sea control, commercial shipping, and blue-water naval power capable of dominating strategic chokepoints:
  - Strait of Malacca
  - Taiwan Strait
  - Suez Canal & Bab el-Mandeb
  - Strait of Hormuz
  - Gibraltar & Panama Canal
- **Modern Geopolitical Relevance**: Underpins US Navy Carrier Strike Group forward posture and China's anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) missile networks designed to deny maritime access within the First Island Chain (Mahan 1890).


## 3. Graham Allison's Thucydides Trap Empirical Framework (Theoretical Frameworks)

Graham Allison's Belfer Center study at Harvard University operationalized historical hegemonic shifts over 500 years, evaluating 16 case studies where a rising power threatened to displace a ruling power (Allison 2017):

| Historical Epoch | Rising Power | Ruling Power | Structural Outcome | Key Conflict Catalyst / Settlement |
| :---: | :----: | :-----: | :------: | :---: |
| **Late 15th Century** | Kingdom of Spain | Kingdom of Portugal | **No War** | Treaty of Tordesillas (1494); Papal arbitration of New World |
| **Mid 17th Century** | Kingdom of England | Dutch Republic | **WAR** | Anglo-Dutch Wars (1652–1674); commercial shipping disputes |
| **Late 18th Century** | United Kingdom | Kingdom of France | **WAR** | Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815); struggle for continental hegemony |
| **Late 19th Century** | German Empire | United Kingdom | **WAR** | World War I (1914–1918); Anglo-German naval arms race |
| **Early 20th Century** | United States | United Kingdom | **No War** | Peaceful financial transition post-WWI/WWII; shared alliance |
| **Late 20th Century** | Soviet Union | United States | **No War** | Cold War containment; nuclear deterrence & economic collapse |
| **Early 21st Century** | China | United States | **Ongoing Friction** | Trade, Tech, and Capital Warfronts; Taiwan Strait friction |

Allison concludes that 12 of the 16 historical cases resulted in kinetic war. Escalation develops across **Five Strategic Warfronts**: (1) Trade, (2) Technology, (3) Geopolitical/Influence, (4) Capital/Financial, and (5) Kinetic Military (Allison 2017).


## 4. Synthesis & Cross-Paradigm Analytical Framework (2026) (Theoretical Frameworks)

The contemporary geopolitical landscape validates a hybrid synthesis of these paradigms:
- **Security & Geopolitics**: Follows **Offensive Realism** and **Power Transition Theory**, as US-China competition intensifies over regional maritime influence (Mearsheimer 2001; Organski & Kugler 1980).
- **Macro-Economy & Finance**: Follows **World-Systems Analysis** and **Hegemonic Stability Theory**, as US financialization drives debt monetization while global trade fragmentations validate Braudel's "financial autumn" (Braudel 1984; Kindleberger 1973).
- **Geographic Logistics**: Re-enacts the **Mackinder vs. Mahan** friction, pitting Eurasian land-based trade corridors (BRI/SCO) against Western blue-water maritime containment (Mackinder 1904; Mahan 1890).
