# Kondratiev Waves: Does Media Sentiment Track Long-Wave Phases?

*Rewritten from the evidence up in Step 13 (2026-10-17). The previous version, built on invented "media sentiment" tables and an undefined sentiment formula, is preserved at `archive/step13_originals/Kondratiev Waves/Media_Analysis/Media Analysis Deep Dive.md.orig`.*

## Executive Summary

The earlier version of this report presented precise sentiment scores for the 1920s, the 1990s and the 2020s that had no source. This rewrite asks what the research literature can actually show about media, narratives and long waves. Three bodies of evidence are solid, but they work on short horizons. Tetlock (2007) shows that pessimism in a daily *Wall Street Journal* column predicts downward pressure on stock prices, followed by a reversion to fundamentals, over days. Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016) build an index of economic policy uncertainty from newspaper coverage that spikes around elections, wars, the Lehman failure and fiscal fights, and that foreshadows falls in investment, output and employment. Shiller (2017) argues that popular narratives help explain the 1920-21 depression, the Great Depression and the Great Recession, but says such narratives are "difficult to study in a scientific manner". None of these studies tests whether media sentiment follows 40-60 year Kondratiev phases, and the data cannot do so yet: consistent newspaper text covers at most two or three long waves. The evidence supports media and narratives as short-run amplifiers of booms and busts. It gives no support to a long-wave sentiment cycle.

## Question and scope

**Question.** Is there measurable evidence that financial media sentiment or popular economic narratives move with Kondratiev long-wave phases, as opposed to shorter market and business cycles?

**Scope.** Peer-reviewed quantitative studies of media content and markets or the macroeconomy, and Shiller's narrative-economics framework. US and English-language press. Not covered: social media, and non-financial cultural "zeitgeist" claims.

## Methodology

1. Identify the main quantitative methods for measuring media sentiment and narratives: word-count sentiment, newspaper-frequency indices and narrative epidemiology.
2. For each, record what it has been shown to predict and over what horizon.
3. Ask whether any study tests a 40-60 year pattern, and what data would be needed.

## Empirical Evidence

| Study | Data | Main finding | Horizon |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Tetlock (2007), *Journal of Finance* | Daily content of a popular *Wall Street Journal* column | High media pessimism predicts downward pressure on prices, then reversion to fundamentals; unusually high or low pessimism predicts high trading volume | Days |
| Baker, Bloom & Davis (2016), *Quarterly Journal of Economics* | Frequency of newspaper articles on economic policy uncertainty, validated by human reading of 12,000 articles | Index spikes at tight elections, the Gulf Wars, 9/11, Lehman's failure and the 2011 debt-ceiling dispute; uncertainty shocks foreshadow falls in investment, output and employment | Months to a few years |
| Shiller (2017), *American Economic Review* (presidential address) | Narrative "epidemics" | Popular narratives "go viral" and help explain the 1920-21 depression, the Great Depression and the Great Recession; the topic is "difficult to study in a scientific manner" | Episodes of a few years |

### What this evidence supports

- Media content carries information about investor sentiment that moves prices in the short run. In Tetlock's results the effect then reverses, which is what one would expect if media mood amplifies swings rather than reflecting new fundamentals.
- Newspaper-based indices can measure uncertainty in a way that predicts real economic activity.
- Booms and busts, including those the long-wave literature treats as turning points (1929, 2007-2009), had strong popular narratives. Shiller treats these narratives as causes worth studying, not just symptoms.

### What it does not support

- A sentiment cycle with a 40-60 year period. No study cited here, and none known to this report, tests one.
- Precise historical sentiment scores for the 1920s or 1990s. These would need a consistent text corpus and a stated method. The previous version of this report gave neither.

### Why a long-wave test is hard

A 50-year cycle needs several repetitions to detect. Even newspaper archives that reach back into the 19th century cover only two or three long waves, and coverage and digitisation are uneven over that span. Language also drifts: the same words carry different tone in 1925 and 2025, so a fixed dictionary will mismeasure sentiment across decades.

## Competing views and counterarguments

- **The case for a long-wave narrative cycle.** Perez's framework predicts a shift in "common sense" during each revolution: euphoria in the installation frenzy, disillusion after the crash, then a new consensus in deployment. Shiller's work makes it plausible that such shifts can be measured in text. A future study could test whether optimism about new technology peaks before each Perez crash. That is a reasonable hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Media as mirror, not driver.** A competing view holds that media mostly reports what is already happening, so any correlation with cycles reflects the economy, not the media. Tetlock's evidence cuts against a pure-mirror view in the short run, since pessimism predicts price moves that later reverse, but it does not settle the question at long horizons.
- **Survivorship in historical narratives.** Stories that "explain" past crises are selected after the fact. Many viral narratives never preceded a crash.

## Limitations

- Three anchor studies represent large literatures. Later work on text-based sentiment (machine-learning classifiers, broader corpora) is not reviewed here.
- The studies use US, English-language sources. Long-wave theory claims global reach.
- Findings are taken from the published abstracts and bibliographic records of each paper (via Crossref). Detailed estimates were not re-checked against the full texts.
- No new measurement is attempted, because the repository holds no text corpus.

## Conclusion

Media sentiment and economic narratives are real, measurable influences on markets and the economy over days, months and episodes of a few years. There is no evidence yet that they follow Kondratiev long-wave phases, and current archives are too short to test it properly. For the Convergence model, media indicators such as policy-uncertainty indices or measures of technology optimism can serve as real-time gauges of fragility during the AI investment boom. They should not be described as evidence of a long-wave phase.
