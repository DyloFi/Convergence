# Kondratiev Waves: Can Long-Wave Turning Points Be Recognised When They Happen?

*Rewritten from the evidence up in Step 13 (2026-10-17). The previous version, a chronological table of events with boilerplate descriptions, is preserved at `archive/step13_originals/Kondratiev Waves/Key_Events/Key Events Deep Dive.md.orig`.*

## Executive Summary

Long-wave accounts are built around key events: the "big bang" that starts a technological revolution and the crash that marks its turning point. The Convergence synthesis uses the same idea forwards, treating current events as signs of a coming turning point. This report asks whether such events can be recognised as turning points at the time, or only afterwards. The record points to afterwards. The canonical big bang of the ICT revolution, Intel's 4004 microprocessor (1971, 2,300 transistors), had a contemporary rival claim in Garrett AiResearch's MP944 chip set (1970), and its significance became clear only years later. At the 1929 market peak, the leading economist Irving Fisher said stocks had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau". Alan Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" warning came on 5 December 1996, more than three years before the Nasdaq peaked in March 2000. Even after the fact, official dating takes time: the NBER announced the December 2007 business-cycle peak on 1 December 2008. Key events are useful for organising history. They are a poor basis for real-time timing, and the Convergence model should use measurable indicators instead.

## Question and scope

**Question.** Were the events that long-wave theory treats as inflection points (technological big bangs, speculative peaks, the start of depressions) recognisable as such when they happened?

**Scope.** Four well-documented events: the 1971 microprocessor, the 1929 stock-market peak, the 1996-2000 dot-com boom and the December 2007 business-cycle peak. For each, the evidence is what contemporaries said or did, set against what is known now. This report does not re-tell the crises (see `Case_Studies/Case Studies Deep Dive.md`) or compare wave datings (see `History/History Deep Dive.md`).

## Methodology

For each event I record (1) why long-wave accounts treat it as an inflection point, (2) a dated contemporary statement or action showing how it was seen at the time, and (3) the lag between the event and its general recognition. Only primary or official sources are used for the contemporary evidence: the Computer History Museum, Federal Reserve speeches and histories, and NBER announcements.

## Empirical Evidence

### 1. A big bang with a rival: the microprocessor (1970-1971)

Perez dates the ICT revolution from the announcement of the Intel microprocessor in 1971. The Computer History Museum records that the 4004 put 2,300 transistors into a low-cost 16-pin package. It also records that in 1970 Garrett AiResearch had designed the MP944 chip set for the F-14A's air data computer, a rival claim to the first microprocessor. A "big bang" is therefore partly a choice made in hindsight about which of several near-simultaneous innovations to name. Its economic importance showed up only as microprocessors spread through the 1970s and 1980s.

### 2. A peak called a plateau (1929)

The Federal Reserve History account of the crash notes that after prices peaked, Irving Fisher, one of the most respected economists of the day, said stock prices had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau." Within weeks the Dow had lost almost half its value. It fell 89% by mid-1932.

### 3. A warning years too early (1996-2000)

On 5 December 1996, Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan asked: "how do we know when irrational exuberance has unduly escalated asset values, which then become subject to unexpected and prolonged contractions?" The Nasdaq Composite did not peak until 10 March 2000, at 5,048.62, more than three years later and after a large further rise. A correct diagnosis of excess gave no usable timing.

### 4. A turning point dated a year late (2007-2008)

The NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee met on 28 November 2008. On 1 December 2008 it announced that a peak in economic activity had occurred in December 2007, ending a 73-month expansion. The turning point of the Great Recession was officially recognised about 12 months after it happened.

## Competing views and counterarguments

- **Some people saw it coming.** In every episode some contemporaries warned of excess, and Greenspan's question was correct in substance. Defenders of long-wave thinking argue that the framework tells you *what kind* of turning point to expect (a crash after heavy infrastructure investment), even if not *when*.
- **Hindsight bias.** The opposite view is that naming events as big bangs or turning points after the fact makes history look more regular than it was. Many warnings that came to nothing, and many innovations that went nowhere, are not on any list.
- **Better real-time tools.** Modern indicators (credit growth, the gap between capital spending and revenue, valuation measures, yield curves) give earlier and more testable warnings than events chosen by analogy. They also produce false alarms. That makes them more honest, not less useful.

## Limitations

- Four events cannot establish a general rule. They were chosen because their contemporary record is well documented.
- Fisher's remark is quoted from the Federal Reserve History essay, not the original newspaper report.
- The Nasdaq peak figure comes from a secondary summary (Wikipedia). It is widely reported.
- "Recognition" is hard to date exactly. The lags in the table are approximate and partly a matter of judgement.
- The sample leaves out warnings that proved wrong and innovations that faded. Including them would strengthen the conclusion that real-time event-reading is unreliable, but this report has not measured how often that happens.

## Conclusion

The events at the heart of long-wave histories were mostly recognised as turning points after the fact. Contemporaries either disputed their importance (the microprocessor), misread them (Fisher in 1929), saw the excess years too early (Greenspan in 1996), or confirmed them only with a long delay (the NBER in 2008). For 2026-2035, the Convergence model should not claim to be at a specific long-wave inflection point because of particular events. It should name the indicators that would mark one and track them.
